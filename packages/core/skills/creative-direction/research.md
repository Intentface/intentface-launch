# How Pros Make Product Launch & Reveal Videos: A Craft Guide (Ideation → Script → Boards → Reveals → Pacing)

The strongest launch videos come from studios that settle one claim and one visual idea before they design a single frame. After that, every shot, cut and sound either helps land that claim or gets cut. Studios that are good at this, such as Giant Ant, Ordinary Folk, ManvsMachine and in-house teams like Figma's and Wix's, spend most of their effort on the message, the boards and the timing. Polish comes last. What makes a video feel dynamic is controlled contrast (fast against slow, dense against empty, loud against silent) set to a planned energy curve. Effects don't do it.

## TL;DR

- **Decide the "one thing" first, then find a visual metaphor for it.** Giant Ant's Jay Grandin frames the job as finding "the nugget, why does anyone care? What should they feel?" Everything Design says a launch film's job is "not to explain every feature" but "to carry the single sharpest claim of the launch in motion."
- **Plan in cheap media before you animate.** Go script, then beat sheet, then thumbnail boards, then 2–3 styleframes, then an animatic timed to temp music, and only then animate. Changes are cheap at the board and animatic stage and expensive after. Apple's own guidance on app previews is to "make some kind of outline or storyboard… start working with the timing here… You don't want to get bogged down in the production stage."
- **For software, rebuild the UI as designed motion instead of screen-recording it.** Simplify the UI to the hero interaction, direct the eye with camera moves and focus, give the cursor intent, and cut or stage reveals on musical beats. Save one big "hold" moment for the payoff.

## Key Findings

1. **The idea matters more than the execution, and good studios protect the idea.** Grandin warns that briefs have become "really reference based, people see a thing and they want that thing. But it's all the stuff we've seen like 100 times, and I think it perpetuates this echo chamber." His creative-direction job is to take an open "we can do anything" brief and put it "into, you know, a smaller box that other people can kind of grab hold of and run with."
2. **Audio is part of the design from the start, not something added at the end.** Ordinary Folk: "Sound, score, and VO are things we're considering from the start, and at every step… Once we have an animatic, we let our amazing composers do their thing." They have even run personal projects where "the music drives the design and animation."
3. **In-house teams now design the product, the brand and the film in parallel.** Wix Motion Studio's Elior Karmani on the Symphony launch: "The brand, the product and the film were all being developed in parallel." On Harmony: "We wanted the product to feel embedded in Angelica's world, not placed beside it." Figma's Ben Hill: "Motion influences design, and design influences motion."
4. **Founders who went viral put clarity ahead of cinema.** In a16z speedrun's case studies, Nico Christie, cofounder and CBO of Fundamental Research Labs, says "No one gives a shit about a cinematic launch video… Make videos that make people think 'these guys are going to make so much money'." His Shortcut video "earned 4.1M impressions" on X and ">5k likes" on LinkedIn, and a follow-up "available now" video earned "another 3.5M impressions." Siddharth Ahuja (Claude×Blender MCP, 23 seconds, "No talking") credits "show over tell." But Daylight's Anjan Katta argues that "most launch videos have no heart or craft," and that tasteful craft was the differentiator. His two team members put in "100 hour weeks for the few weeks of making the launch video." Weber Wong (FLORA) says "advertising is back… People are ready to be seduced again." **The takeaway: pick a register (raw demo, or crafted film) on purpose, based on the claim. Don't average the two.**
5. **There is no public, craft-level write-up of how Linear, Stripe, Vercel, Raycast, Arc or Framer make their launch films.** Their motion teams mostly show up in job posts. Vercel's asks for someone who can "define the rhythm, pacing, and energy" and "use motion to clarify, not complicate". The best primary sources are studio interviews, Apple's WWDC app-preview session, and Figma's and Wix's in-house write-ups.

## Details

### 1. Ideation & concept: finding the one thing

**Start with the brief questions Giant Ant asks every client.** According to Motion Hatch's write-up of the Grandin interview, these are: *Who are we going to talk to and why? Why should they care? What do you want them to feel? What kind of association do you want them to have with your brand, and why? What action do you want them to take after watching?* Answer all five in one page before anyone opens a design tool.

**Compress the answers into a single claim sentence.** Everything Design's test: the film should be "the most memorable expression of a claim that already runs through every other launch surface." If the homepage headline, the deck and the video don't say the same sentence, the video will "show up late, off-message, and forgettable." Fundamental's Nico Christie gives a founder-side test for the claim: "The story should seem obvious in hindsight to your target customer… do they say 'I can't believe this didn't exist before?'" Work backwards from that story "at least three weeks out."

**Map the claim to a visual metaphor or "device".** A practical way to do it:
- Write the claim as a verb: *Linear makes work flow*, *Daylight is less*, *Shortcut is superhuman at spreadsheets*.
- List 10–20 physical or graphic analogues for that verb: flow (liquid, conveyor, a line that never breaks), less (subtraction, whitespace, a dimming light), speed (blur, a time-lapse, a stopwatch), control (a dial, a lever, grids snapping into place).
- Pick **one recurring graphic device** that can carry the whole piece. Art of Styleframe lists "the recurring graphic device that will carry through the piece" as one of the five things every styleframe must lock, alongside palette, type, lighting/depth and composition. ManvsMachine's campaign work shows the payoff of a system. For Squarespace they fixed "type treatment, edit structure, graphic compositions, border and grid overlay, lighting, art direction, and pace" so that three films read as one set.
- Let the material and feel follow from what the product feels like to use. ManvsMachine's reMarkable Paper Pro film deliberately "introduced handheld camera movements and played with the fidelity of our 3D renders" with "grainy, 'low' quality and shaky textural 3D product shots," because "the product has unrivaled tactility, it's as much about the feeling as it is the technology." A tactile product got tactile camera language.

**Choose the look by working backwards from the feeling.** Grandin: "we always like to start with how we want something to look or feel, and work backwards to the technique from there." Ordinary Folk's process page says the same about the design phase: "Is it 2D, 3D, Vector, Cut-out, or hand-drawn? There are endless ways… but only one that's perfect for you."

**Decide the launch format mix up front.** Everything Design lists four formats: a **teaser/announcement**, a **hero launch film**, a **product reveal**, and a **founder/narrative film**. "Most launches use two or three of these, cut from the same core shoot." Plan the cutdowns (long-form, 30s paid, vertical, silent captioned loop) *before* production.

**Pitch hard, and less often.** When Giant Ant pitches, they "do a storyboard. We'll often like rewrite their script. We will like make a bunch of style frames. We'll make an animation test." Grandin says they now "pitch hard" but "pitch less": where they "used to be… making kind of like crappy pitches really often," now "instead we do like maybe a quarter of those," because otherwise it becomes "a styling exercise." For an internal team, the lesson is to test 2–3 genuinely different concepts at storyboard-plus-one-styleframe fidelity. Don't debate a dozen moodboards.

### 2. Scriptwriting for launch videos

**Pick the spine that fits your claim.** Three structures cover most launch videos:

| Structure | Beats | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem → Reveal → Proof → Payoff** | Hook (pain or bold claim) → name the tension → reveal product → 2–3 proof moments → end card/CTA | Category education needed; new behaviour |
| **Manifesto / Thesis** | Worldview line → "so we built" → product glimpses → vision line → logo | Company launches, raises, recruiting (Goodfire's Ember video came with its $50M Series A and "was a flex, a manifesto, and a strong recruiting motion all at once"; OpenSesame) |
| **Pure demo / Show-don't-tell** | Instant product → escalating use cases → "and it can also…" → CTA | Product whose output is the wow (Claude×Blender, Shortcut) |

Jellypod's blog post "How I Made a Launch Video in 24 Hours with Jitter: A Marketer's Guide" is a marketer's account of a launch video made "in just a day" by a self-described non-designer. Its script follows a common six-beat flow: **Hook → What is it → Proof → The Why → Suspense → CTA**, with the song as "the emotional heartbeat" chosen to build "energy where it mattered most."

**Hooks that work (from the a16z case studies):**
- *Provocation:* FLORA opens with "Current AI creative tools are built by non-creatives, for other non-creatives to feel creative."
- *Understatement:* Daylight's "It's not here to change your life. But it might change the way you think about screens," followed by a list of "less… less distraction, less addiction, less eye strain, less blue light."
- *Hard number:* Shortcut's hook was "10x faster than humans."

**Length guidance (a synthesis, not a rule):**
- **6–15s:** one claim, one visual, one logo. Used for teasers and pre-launch "glimpses."
- **30s:** one claim plus 2–3 proof beats. This is Apple's app-preview length, and a common paid cut.
- **60–90s:** the hero film. Everything Design says "the hero cut usually runs 60 to 90 seconds."
- **~2 min:** only for demo-heavy launches. Christie's was "two minutes, no frills, 50%+ product walk-through," and a16z speedrun notes he is "only on-screen for about 15 seconds before diving into the product demo."

**VO vs. on-screen text vs. music-only.**
- **VO** carries narrative and emotion. Daylight's "quiet narrator" suits the understated register.
- **Kinetic text-on-screen** suits feed autoplay with sound off. Budget reading time. Apple: "If you're going to put text up on the screen, you need to put it up there long enough for people to read it."
- **Music-only / no talking** works when the product's output is self-evidently impressive (Ahuja's "No talking, short and straight to the point").

**Writing tight copy.** Write picture and words side by side, never words alone. Clickstrike describes scripts "written with picture and words specified together, then scored second by second against a retention rubric… Any second that does not earn the one after it gets rewritten or cut." Practical rules:
- Keep on-screen lines to 3–6 words.
- Give each beat one idea.
- Use verbs over adjectives.
- Let the visual carry any line it can, and delete that line from the VO.
- Ordinary Folk's script goal is one that "sounds so much like you, you'll think you wrote it yourself." Voice consistency beats cleverness.

### 3. Storyboarding & animatics

**The professional pipeline, and what each stage settles:**

| Stage | Answers | Fidelity |
|---|---|---|
| Moodboard | What feeling (others' work) | Collected images |
| Beat sheet | What happens each ~2–5s, and the energy level | Text table |
| Thumbnails / storyboard | Framing, motion, on-screen text, and how each beat hands off | Rough sketches |
| Styleframes | What the finished frame looks like | Final quality; "two or three for a short spot, usually the opening, a mid-point and the end card" |
| Animatic | Timing, rhythm, music sync | Boards on a timeline with temp VO/music |

Art of Styleframe's distinction is useful: "A storyboard settles what happens. A moodboard collects references someone else made. A styleframe is the only one of the three that commits to how the finished work will actually look." It adds that "a rough styleframe is worse than none: it invites notes on the wrong things." It also says: "If every scene needs its own styleframe… the art direction has not settled yet."

**Board the transitions, not just the shots.** Clickstrike boards "how each beat hands off to the next." They call boards "the second and last cheap place to make structural changes. Once animation starts, the cost of moving a beat rises sharply." Sketch the last frame of shot A next to the first frame of shot B. That is where match cuts and eye-trace transitions get designed.

**Build a beat sheet with an energy column.** For each beat, record time in and out, the visual, VO/text, SFX/music cue, and energy on a 1–5 scale. Plot the energy numbers. A good launch curve goes: a strong spike in the first 1–3s (the hook), a dip into tension (the problem), a ramp up, a *silent or near-silent breath* just before the reveal, the peak at the reveal, a proof section with small oscillations, and a settled, confident end card. If the plot is flat, the video will feel monotonous no matter how polished it is.

**Make the animatic and time it to temp music early.** Ordinary Folk bring in the composer "once we have an animatic." If you use library music, lock a temp track at the animatic stage. Mark its downbeats, drops and breaks, and snap your beat boundaries to them. Apple's WWDC app-preview session (Paul Turner) put this bluntly: "Planning is the biggest part… It could be on a napkin… start working with the timing here. This is the time in the planning stage."

**Designers who can't draw** can use screenshots, grey boxes and camera arrows. On storyboarding a School of Motion piece, David Ariew says his wife drew the boards because he is "really terrible at drawing," and that the rough sketches still "helped" composition heavily.

### 4. Reveal techniques & ideas (the toolbox)

**A taxonomy of reveals: pick one hero reveal and 2–4 supporting ones.**

| Reveal | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Tease → reveal** | Show fragments (edges, reflections, a glyph, a sliver of UI, a sound) before the whole | Hero moment; hardware silhouettes; big UI redesigns |
| **Mask / wipe reveal** | Product appears through a shape, a line, text counters, or the previous shot's geometry | Logo and title builds; transitions between features |
| **Light sweep / rim light** | A moving light defines edges before the form is lit | Hardware and "premium" reveals; also glass-UI panels |
| **Push-in / zoom-through** | Camera flies into a detail (a button, a pixel, a letterform) that opens into the next scene | Going from brand world into product; feature-to-feature |
| **Pull-back** | Start on a macro detail, pull back to show the full product or system | "It's bigger than you think" moments |
| **Match cut** | Similar shape, position or motion carries across a cut | Linking metaphor to product (the metaphor's circle becomes the UI's avatar) |
| **Morph / kinetic transition** | Scene A's geometry physically transforms into scene B | Abstract to concrete; problem to solution |
| **Assemble / build-up** | UI components fly in and snap into a layout on beats | "Everything in one place" claims |
| **Before/after snap** | Chaotic state resolves into ordered state | Productivity/"less" claims |
| **Type-driven reveal** | Kinetic words collide with, push or become UI | Text-forward launches |

**Match cuts, done correctly.** School of Motion (Jacob Richardson and Ryan Plummer) gives the core rule: "continue the momentum of the previous subject's movement… if you have a twelve frame move and decide to cut on frame six, pick-up the next shot on frame seven." They describe match cuts on "shape, color, or movement" and note that "the subconscious auto-completes the story, that subject A and B are equal." That last point is why match cuts are the best tool for landing a *metaphor → product* equivalence. SoM's related "dynamic zoom" transition pushes into shapes and cuts "at their fastest movement," hiding the cut inside motion blur.

**Hardware reveals (Apple-style) in one sentence each:**
- Backlight and edge light so the form is drawn by highlights.
- Use slow, motivated camera moves where "every move should reveal something new about the product."
- Use macro detail passes. Manfrotto's recreation moved a light panel on a linear slider to "draw lines of light onto the phone."
- Use restraint. The Render Edge guide's rule of thumb: "If the motion feels slightly slower than you think it should be, you're probably in the right zone."

**Order of introduction (what to show first).** A reliable sequence is **world/tension → the one hero interaction → proof of breadth → identity**:
1. The feeling or the problem, often abstract. Wix's approach is to focus on "moments of progress rather than on individual features or tasks."
2. The single most magical interaction, in full, at readable speed.
3. Two or three quick variations or use cases to prove it isn't a one-trick demo.
4. Logo, claim line, and CTA.

Hold back the name and logo until the viewer wants it. Show the most valuable interaction, not the first screen of onboarding. The advids analysis described in Caveats found that some SaaS teasers (it names Arc, Linear) delay the full UI to about the midpoint, while others (Stripe, Vercel) show it within about 6 seconds. Its stated rule of thumb is to delay if the category needs education and front-load if you compete on speed. Treat that as a hypothesis (see Caveats).

**Teasers and "tease then reveal" in a campaign.** Everything Design's teaser format builds "anticipation before the date." Figma's Config 2025 opener (with studio Relay) "had the dual purpose of building excitement… and teasing some of the launches to be announced," using "flashes of grids, gradients, textures, and code" to hint at products without showing them.

### 5. Making it dynamic: rhythm, contrast, energy, and what to cut

**Principles that transfer from animation and editing to launch videos:**

- **Contrast is the engine.** Rhythm without contrast turns monotonous. The pattern for a montage is: "use three fast cuts to establish energy, hold one satisfying shot for two beats, then return to faster cutting." If every cut lands on the beat, the viewer can predict the timeline. "If one key moment resists the beat, the viewer pays attention." Put your reveal on that off-pattern moment, or on a beat that follows a drop-out of silence.
- **Offset and overlap, never unison.** The biggest tell of amateur motion is everything moving at once. The standard craft principle: let secondary elements follow primary ones a few frames later. Text should arrive "a fraction after shapes lock," because "if everything fires at once, it looks like a PowerPoint transition." Stagger UI cards, labels and cursor arrivals.
- **Easing carries emotion.** Heavy ease-outs (fast in, long settle) feel premium and confident. Snappy, springy motion feels playful. Linear motion reads as mechanical, so use it only for deliberate effects like tickers, marquees and constant camera drift. Pick one "motion personality" for the whole piece and keep it.
- **Anticipation before big moves.** A small counter-move, a pause, or a dip in the music before a reveal makes the reveal land harder. In the energy curve this is the "breath" before the peak.
- **Motion continuity across cuts.** If something exits screen right, the next thing should enter from the left, or keep moving the same direction. That keeps kinetic energy flowing through scene changes.
- **Sound design is half the pacing.** Put a whoosh or riser into every major move and a hit or click on every snap. Use silence or near-silence right before the hero reveal. Sync UI clicks and typing to the rhythm. Apple's Turner singled out a preview where "they synced up the music." Plan the music's structure (intro, build, drop, outro) against your energy curve.

**Keeping it tight: a cutting checklist.**
- Cut any shot that doesn't advance the one claim, however beautiful it is.
- Cut any feature that needs its own explanation. Save it for docs or a follow-up video.
- Cut duplicate proofs. Two examples of the same capability become one.
- Trim the heads and tails of every shot. Enter late, leave early.
- Cut VO lines the picture already says.
- Watch at 1x, sound off, on a phone. If the claim isn't clear by the end, the text layer is failing.
- Watch only the first 3 seconds. If they wouldn't stop your own scroll, rebuild the hook.

The tightness standard, from Siddharth Ahuja: "respecting their time and attention," and "if you have goosebumps, then your audience will most likely too."

### 6. Showing software UI in launch videos

**Stylized over literal, almost always.** Launch-video agency Flowjam's pitch is a fair summary of current practice: "We rebuild your UI as motion so it looks far better than a screen recording ever could." Simplified UI also ages better. What A Story notes that simplified UI means a later button-color change doesn't make "your $10k video… obsolete."

**How to simplify UI for video:**
1. **Reduce to the hero path.** Keep only the elements involved in the interaction. Grey out or remove chrome, sidebars and secondary panels, or replace them with blocks.
2. **Enlarge what matters.** Crop tight, increase type scale, and bump contrast. App-mockup guidance: "Check contrast at the final playback size, not only in the editor."
3. **Clean the data.** Use believable, on-brand, short sample content. Remove "personal data, notifications, cursor noise, and accidental overlays."
4. **Choose one camera and device move per shot.** "Use one primary device or camera movement to establish attention, then one supporting movement to reveal the product state… Avoid constant rotation, fast parallax, and deep perspective when the viewer needs to read the screen."
5. **Use UI-in-3D-space for transitions and moments of awe, and go flat for reading.** Tilt panels in space to move between features. Straighten them when text must be read.

**Focus the eye.** Use the following, one at a time:
- **Push-in / crop:** zoom into the region of interest.
- **Depth of field / blur:** blur everything except the active component.
- **Dim-and-spotlight:** darken surrounding UI.
- **Isolation:** lift the component out of the UI onto its own plane.
- **Kinetic callout:** a short label that lands next to the change.

**Cursor choreography.** Treat the cursor as a character with intent:
- It moves on smooth arcs, not straight robotic lines.
- It decelerates into targets.
- It pauses a beat before clicking, and the click has a visual and audio accent.
- It never wanders.

Apple's app-preview guidance applies directly: "Go slow… If your customer can't follow along with what you're doing, they're not going to understand… Use intentional moves, smooth moves… rehearse it, retake it." The FLORA launch shows the founder-demo version: "The camera is zoomed in, closely following the on-screen mouse." According to a16z speedrun, it made viewers want to ask for access, "which is exactly what hundreds of creatives did." Often the most premium choice is to *remove* the cursor and let UI respond on its own, stepping state changes on the beat.

**Honesty with cuts.** Apple recommends jump cuts but warns that "in jump cutting, you can imply that your app does something that your app doesn't actually do." Use a dissolve or fade-through-colour when you are compressing time, so viewers don't read it as instant.

**Feature highlight patterns that recur in software launch films:**
- **Hero loop:** one interaction shown fully, then replayed faster at the end as a callback.
- **Triptych / grid burst:** three to nine quick feature tiles on consecutive beats, proving breadth after the hero.
- **Type-meets-UI:** words land and physically push panels. Advids reports this in Framer and Linear teasers, though that is secondhand.
- **Speed proof:** a real-time counter, a side-by-side comparison, or time-lapse against a clock.
- **Keyboard-shortcut chips:** overlay the keys pressed for keyboard-first tools.
- **Story-embedded UI:** the product lives inside a character's world. Wix's aim was "without turning AI itself into the visual hero" and to make "agents feel active."

**AI products specifically.** Karmani's Symphony note is the most useful principle found for AI launches. Show the *outcomes and progress* agents create, and don't make abstract "AI" visuals (orbs, neural nets, sparkles) the hero. Pair that with Ahuja's "show good use cases… it's wrong to assume your audience sees the potential you see."

### 8. Existing prompts, skills and frameworks for AI agents

Most public "motion skills" for AI agents are **tooling or UI-focused**, not creative-direction craft:
- **LottieFiles motion-design-skill** codifies timing, easing, choreography and Disney principles, but "adapted for UI." It is useful for easing and stagger tables, not for scripting. https://github.com/lottiefiles/motion-design-skill
- **farhan-syah/motion-video-skill** has the agent "write a direction (concept, look, beats, sound)" before building HTML/CSS scenes. It's the closest to a director workflow, but it is tied to a renderer. https://github.com/farhan-syah/motion-video-skill
- **iart-ai/motion-skills** is 50 skills including kinetic typography and beat-synced cuts, mostly execution-oriented. https://github.com/iart-ai/motion-skills
- **aicontentskills/ai-video-storyboard-skill** outputs a shared visual theme, a 6–18 shot list with "purpose, composition, camera move," audio direction and a "why this works" rationale. The structure is good; it targets generative video. https://github.com/aicontentskills/ai-video-storyboard-skill
- **smixs/visual-skills** adds a "six-point dramaturgy check" and a "three-detail audit on every shot," drawing on film-editing theory (Murch, blocking, montage). https://github.com/smixs/visual-skills

**Verdict:** nothing public cleanly codifies *launch-video creative direction*. The better move is to write your own SKILL.md from this guide. Here is a compact template:

```
# SKILL: Launch Video Creative Director
## 1. Brief (answer all before ideating)
- Audience + why they should care | What should they FEEL | Brand association | Action after watching
- ONE claim sentence (must match homepage headline)
- Register: raw-demo | crafted-film | manifesto  (choose one, justify)
## 2. Concept
- Claim as a verb → 15 visual analogues → pick 1 recurring graphic device
- 3 distinct concepts, each = logline + 1 key frame description + hook line
## 3. Script (picture + words together, two columns)
- Spine: Problem→Reveal→Proof→Payoff | Manifesto | Pure demo
- Hook in ≤3s; on-screen lines ≤6 words; delete any VO the picture says
- Lengths: 15s teaser / 30s paid / 60–90s hero; plan cutdowns + 9:16 now
## 4. Beat sheet (table)
- time | visual | text/VO | SFX/music cue | transition out | energy 1–5
- Energy curve: spike → dip → ramp → BREATH (silence) → PEAK reveal → oscillate → settle
## 5. Reveal plan
- 1 hero reveal (tease→reveal / light sweep / push-through / match cut / assemble)
- 2–4 supporting transitions; every transition boarded as last-frame/first-frame pair
## 6. UI direction
- Rebuild UI simplified to hero path; enlarge; clean data; one camera move per shot
- Flat when reading, 3D between features; cursor = intentional arcs, pause-then-click, or no cursor
- Honest cuts: dissolve when compressing time
## 7. Motion + sound rules
- One motion personality (easing family) for whole piece; stagger/offset, never unison
- Cuts on beats, but put the hero reveal off-pattern or after a drop-out
- SFX on every snap/click; lock temp music at animatic stage
## 8. Tightness QA
- Every second earns the next | sound-off phone test | first-3-seconds test | cut duplicate proofs
```

## Recommendations

1. **For your next SaaS/AI launch, default to a 45–75s crafted film plus a 15s teaser and a vertical cut.** Use the Problem → Reveal → Proof → Payoff spine, and put the most magical interaction in the reveal.
2. **Run the process in this order and don't skip stages:** one-page brief → claim sentence → 3 concepts → two-column script → beat sheet with energy column → boards with transition pairs → 2–3 styleframes (open, mid, end card) → animatic on temp music → animation → sound pass. Most of the quality is decided before animation.
3. **Invest disproportionately in three moments:** the first 3 seconds, the hero reveal (with a breath before it), and the end card. Everything else can be simpler.
4. **Rebuild the UI rather than recording it,** and simplify it harder than feels comfortable. If a feature needs explanation, cut it from the launch film.
5. **Encode this as your own agent skill** (template above) and use existing repos only for execution details (easing tables, beat-sync) once direction is locked.
6. **Study with the sound off, then on.** For each reference film, write out its beat sheet and energy curve. That's the fastest way to absorb how Apple-, Linear- and Stripe-style pacing works.

## Caveats

- **There is little primary-source material from Linear, Stripe, Vercel, Raycast, Arc or Framer.** Search found no craft write-ups from their motion teams. Statements here about their pacing come from advids, a video-agency blog. That analysis of seven SaaS teasers (Arc, Linear, Stripe, Vercel, Supabase, Framer) reports percentages like "71% (5/7)" from a tiny sample. It reads like marketing content and should be treated as unverified hypotheses, not findings.

- **The Apple WWDC advice is from 2014 and is about 30-second App Store previews.** The principles carry over, but it isn't about keynote films.
- **The Figma Config quotes came from search snippets** (the Figma blog blocked full-text fetching), so treat exact wording as close but not guaranteed.
- **Several frameworks here (the energy curve, the reveal taxonomy, length bands, and the SKILL.md template) are my synthesis** of the sourced principles and standard animation and editing craft. They are not quoted from any one studio.
