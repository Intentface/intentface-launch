# Creative direction: sources

The research behind SKILL.md: what studios, in-house teams and founders have said about how launch films are made, with quotes and links. SKILL.md is the practice; this file is the evidence and the examples to show the person.

## The one claim and the idea

- **Giant Ant (Jay Grandin)** frames the job as finding "the nugget, why does anyone care? What should they feel?" Motion Hatch's write-up of the interview lists the brief questions they ask every client: who are we talking to and why, why should they care, what should they feel, what association should they have with the brand, and what should they do after watching.
- Grandin warns that briefs have become "really reference based, people see a thing and they want that thing. But it's all the stuff we've seen like 100 times, and I think it perpetuates this echo chamber." Creative direction is taking an open brief and putting it "into, you know, a smaller box that other people can kind of grab hold of and run with."
- On technique: "we always like to start with how we want something to look or feel, and work backwards to the technique from there." Ordinary Folk's process page says the same of design: "Is it 2D, 3D, Vector, Cut-out, or hand-drawn? There are endless ways… but only one that's perfect for you."
- **Everything Design**: a launch film's job is "not to explain every feature" but "to carry the single sharpest claim of the launch in motion", "the most memorable expression of a claim that already runs through every other launch surface". If the homepage, the deck and the video say different things, the video will "show up late, off-message, and forgettable".
- **Nico Christie** (Fundamental Research Labs), on the story: "The story should seem obvious in hindsight to your target customer… do they say 'I can't believe this didn't exist before?'" He works backwards from it "at least three weeks out".
- **Pitching:** Giant Ant "pitch hard" but "pitch less": where they "used to be… making kind of like crappy pitches really often," they now make "maybe a quarter of those", each with a storyboard, a rewritten script, style frames and an animation test, because otherwise it becomes "a styling exercise".

## The recurring device and the look

- **Art of Styleframe** lists "the recurring graphic device that will carry through the piece" as one of five things every styleframe must lock, with palette, type, lighting and depth, and composition.
- **ManvsMachine** fixed "type treatment, edit structure, graphic compositions, border and grid overlay, lighting, art direction, and pace" for Squarespace, so that three films read as one set.
- For the reMarkable Paper Pro film, ManvsMachine "introduced handheld camera movements and played with the fidelity of our 3D renders", with "grainy, 'low' quality and shaky textural 3D product shots", because "the product has unrivaled tactility, it's as much about the feeling as it is the technology."
- **Wix Motion Studio** (Elior Karmani) on Symphony: "The brand, the product and the film were all being developed in parallel." On Harmony: "We wanted the product to feel embedded in Angelica's world, not placed beside it." **Figma's** Ben Hill: "Motion influences design, and design influences motion."

## Register: raw demo or crafted film

From a16z speedrun's "How to Make a Viral Launch Video", six founder case studies:

- **Nico Christie** (Shortcut): "No one gives a shit about a cinematic launch video… Make videos that make people think 'these guys are going to make so much money'." The video "earned 4.1M impressions" on X and ">5k likes" on LinkedIn, and a follow-up "available now" video "another 3.5M impressions". It ran "two minutes, no frills, 50%+ product walk-through"; he is "only on-screen for about 15 seconds before diving into the product demo".
- **Siddharth Ahuja** (Claude×Blender MCP, 23 s): "No talking, short and straight to the point"; "show good use cases… it's wrong to assume your audience sees the potential you see"; "respecting their time and attention"; "if you have goosebumps, then your audience will most likely too."
- **Anjan Katta** (Daylight): "most launch videos have no heart or craft." Two team members put in "100 hour weeks for the few weeks of making the launch video."
- **Weber Wong** (FLORA): "advertising is back… People are ready to be seduced again." The FLORA video's "camera is zoomed in, closely following the on-screen mouse", and it made viewers want to ask for access, "which is exactly what hundreds of creatives did."
- **Goodfire's** Ember video came with its $50M Series A and "was a flex, a manifesto, and a strong recruiting motion all at once."

## Script

- Script spines in use: problem, reveal, proof, payoff; manifesto (worldview, "so we built", glimpses, vision, logo: Goodfire, OpenSesame); pure demo (Claude×Blender, Shortcut).
- **Jellypod**, "How I Made a Launch Video in 24 Hours with Jitter": a marketer's video made "in just a day", on six beats (hook, what it is, proof, the why, suspense, call to action), with the song as "the emotional heartbeat" chosen to build "energy where it mattered most."
- **Hooks** from the a16z cases. Provocation: FLORA's "Current AI creative tools are built by non-creatives, for other non-creatives to feel creative." Understatement: Daylight's "It's not here to change your life. But it might change the way you think about screens," then "less distraction, less addiction, less eye strain, less blue light." Hard number: Shortcut's "10x faster than humans."
- **Length:** Everything Design says "the hero cut usually runs 60 to 90 seconds", and "most launches use two or three" of four formats (teaser, hero film, product reveal, founder film), "cut from the same core shoot". Apple's App Store previews run 30 s.
- **Clickstrike** writes scripts "with picture and words specified together, then scored second by second against a retention rubric… Any second that does not earn the one after it gets rewritten or cut."
- **Ordinary Folk** aim for a script that "sounds so much like you, you'll think you wrote it yourself."
- **Apple**, on text: "If you're going to put text up on the screen, you need to put it up there long enough for people to read it."

## Boards and animatics

- **Art of Styleframe:** "A storyboard settles what happens. A moodboard collects references someone else made. A styleframe is the only one of the three that commits to how the finished work will actually look." "A rough styleframe is worse than none: it invites notes on the wrong things." "If every scene needs its own styleframe… the art direction has not settled yet." Usually "two or three for a short spot, usually the opening, a mid-point and the end card".
- **Clickstrike** boards "how each beat hands off to the next", and calls boards "the second and last cheap place to make structural changes. Once animation starts, the cost of moving a beat rises sharply."
- **Ordinary Folk:** "Sound, score, and VO are things we're considering from the start, and at every step… Once we have an animatic, we let our amazing composers do their thing." Some of their personal projects let "the music drive the design and animation."
- **Apple, WWDC 2014 session 304** (Paul Turner): "Planning is the biggest part… It could be on a napkin… start working with the timing here. This is the time in the planning stage." And: "make some kind of outline or storyboard… You don't want to get bogged down in the production stage."
- **David Ariew** (School of Motion) had his wife draw his boards because he is "really terrible at drawing"; the rough sketches still "helped" the composition.

## Reveals and transitions

- **School of Motion** (Jacob Richardson and Ryan Plummer) on match cuts: "continue the momentum of the previous subject's movement… if you have a twelve frame move and decide to cut on frame six, pick-up the next shot on frame seven." Match on "shape, color, or movement"; "the subconscious auto-completes the story, that subject A and B are equal." Their "dynamic zoom" pushes into shapes and cuts "at their fastest movement", hiding the cut in the blur.
- **Hardware reveals:** a light panel on a linear slider to "draw lines of light onto the phone" (Manfrotto's recreation of an Apple-style shot); "every move should reveal something new about the product"; and The Render Edge's rule of thumb: "If the motion feels slightly slower than you think it should be, you're probably in the right zone."
- **Wix** focuses on "moments of progress rather than on individual features or tasks."
- **Figma's Config 2025 opener** (with Relay) "had the dual purpose of building excitement… and teasing some of the launches to be announced", with "flashes of grids, gradients, textures, and code".
- **advids**, analysing seven SaaS teasers, reports that some (Arc, Linear) hold the full UI back to about the midpoint and others (Stripe, Vercel) show it within about 6 s: hold back if the category needs explaining, show it early if you compete on speed. A small sample; see Caveats.

## Rhythm and contrast

- Montage pattern from editing practice: "use three fast cuts to establish energy, hold one satisfying shot for two beats, then return to faster cutting." When every cut lands on the beat the viewer can predict it; "if one key moment resists the beat, the viewer pays attention."
- Text should arrive "a fraction after shapes lock", because "if everything fires at once, it looks like a PowerPoint transition."
- **Apple's Turner** singled out a preview where "they synced up the music."

## Showing software UI

- **Flowjam** (a launch-video agency): "We rebuild your UI as motion so it looks far better than a screen recording ever could." **What A Story**: with simplified UI, a later button-colour change does not make "your $10k video… obsolete."
- App-mockup guidance: "Check contrast at the final playback size, not only in the editor"; remove "personal data, notifications, cursor noise, and accidental overlays"; "Use one primary device or camera movement to establish attention, then one supporting movement to reveal the product state… Avoid constant rotation, fast parallax, and deep perspective when the viewer needs to read the screen."
- **Apple** on demos: "Go slow… If your customer can't follow along with what you're doing, they're not going to understand… Use intentional moves, smooth moves… rehearse it, retake it." On jump cuts: "in jump cutting, you can imply that your app does something that your app doesn't actually do."
- **Wix** on Symphony, an AI product: show what the agents achieve, "without turning AI itself into the visual hero", and make "agents feel active."
- **Vercel's** motion-designer job post asks for someone who can "define the rhythm, pacing, and energy" and "use motion to clarify, not complicate".

## Caveats

- No craft write-ups from the motion teams at Linear, Stripe, Vercel, Raycast, Arc or Framer were found. What is said here about their pacing comes from advids, a video-agency blog whose analysis of seven teasers reports percentages like "71% (5/7)"; treat it as a hypothesis.
- Several sources are agencies or tool vendors (Clickstrike, Flowjam, What A Story, Everything Design, The Render Edge), and partly self-promotional. Their process observations are kept here, their statistics are not.
- The a16z founders are partly a16z speedrun portfolio companies (Shortcut, FLORA and OpenSesame are disclosed as such). Their accounts are survivor stories, not controlled evidence.
- Apple's advice is from 2014 and about 30-second App Store previews, not keynote films.
- The Figma quotes come from search snippets (the blog blocked full-text fetching), so the exact wording is close but not guaranteed.
- The energy curve, the reveal table, the length bands and the cutting checklist in SKILL.md are a synthesis of these sources and standard editing craft, not quoted from any one studio.
