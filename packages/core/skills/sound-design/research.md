# Sound design: sources

The research behind SKILL.md: how sound designers and sonic-branding teams build effects and audio logos, what ElevenLabs' sound effects can do and allow, and the delivery specs. SKILL.md is the practice, with the commands; this file is the evidence. Numbers in brackets are the sources listed at the end.

## Restraint

- Joel Beckerman (Man Made Music) to NPR, 2014: "the first thing we do is we try to pull all the sound out. We start, really, with a blank slate and then add just the sounds that benefit you."\[68\]
- Google's multisensory team asks of every sound, "Does this action need sound…and why?", treats silence as the audio equivalent of negative space, and holds that "the more often an interaction happens, the less intrusive that sound or haptic should be."\[1\] Google's shutter sound went from melodic to minimal and realistic because melodies on frequent actions were "distracting and confusing."\[1\]
- One well-placed impact is powerful; four in a row is a parody. An impact with no tail sounds small however loud it is. A riser that never resolves into an impact or deliberate silence reads as unfinished. Braams are a trailer cliché.\[14\]
- Whooshes are the most overused sound in marketing; short, light ones suit UI and type, longer sweeps big camera moves.\[13\]
- UI sounds that feel satisfying are short (50 to 200 ms), with a crisp transient and little tail, consistent across the set, and quiet.\[16\]
- A launch film's sound builds a dynamic arc: sparse at the start, intensifying, paying off near the end.\[17\]

## Syncing sound to motion

- Motion designers who start from the track can put transitions on beats.\[18\] Impacts that also land on downbeats reinforce the music instead of fighting it.\[14\]\[19\]
- Starting the next scene's sound a few frames early (a pre-lap) smooths a transition better than a whoosh.\[20\]
- A short drop-out before the final hit makes it land harder than extra layers would.\[19\]
- Placing a whoosh's peak at the fastest frame of the move, and an impact's transient on the frame of contact, are standard editing practice rather than a sourced rule.

## Music, voice and effects together

- Duck music and design elements under voice and title cards.\[15\] The music's low end and a sub hit compete, so dip one when the other plays.\[15\] Frequency-specific ducking tools (Trackspacer, Soothe) push the music down only in the bands an impact occupies, only while it plays.\[21\]
- Material Design: sounds that follow one another "should have related attributes (like timbre, melody, or envelope)."\[29\] Tonal sounds communicate personality, emotion and state changes; atonal ones support motion and a "haptic" feel.\[24\] Unresolved melodies can sound ambiguous.\[24\]

## Layering

- Lon Bender (Formosa Group, who built Netflix's logo) to Twenty Thousand Hertz: "Any sound is made up of four sounds, generally."\[8\]
- BOOM Library: "think in layers (frequency, sustain, tail, transient)" and "don't use too many layers over each other."\[19\] Set the body first as the anchor, tuck the sub under it, add detail on top at a lower level.\[15\]\[28\] Mute layers one at a time; if a layer adds nothing you can hear, delete it.\[28\]
- Anything with a pitch should be in the music's key. Duende Sounds: tonal clashes make a mix feel "slightly off", and audiences "cannot tell you what."\[14\] Google builds its whole product soundscape around the key of G so that "everything feels like it's part of the same system."\[1\]

## Reverb and delay

- Different rooms for every sound make a mix feel like a collage; one or two shared spaces are enough.\[30\]
- Pre-delay of 20 to 60 ms keeps transients and voice intelligible while adding size.\[31\]
- High-pass reverb returns around 150 to 250 Hz against mud, and low-pass around 6 to 9 kHz for a less digital tail.\[30\]\[32\]
- A sidechain compressor on the reverb return, keyed from the voice, lets the tail bloom in the gaps.\[31\]\[33\]
- Tempo-synced delays and decays: a quarter note is 60,000 ÷ BPM ms. The tail should be almost gone before the next strong beat.\[31\]
- Pitfalls: long tails piling up in fast edits, reverb low end masking voice and bass, wide reverbs that collapse in mono.\[30\]

## Premium and cheap

- Fastl and Zwicker: sensory pleasantness generally falls as sharpness (excess high-frequency energy) and roughness (fast amplitude modulation, about 15 to 300 Hz, most sensitive near 70 Hz) rise.\[34\]\[35\]\[67\]
- Octaves, fifths and major triads read as stable and positive. Google's Guided Frame uses a IV, V, I progression that "feels like home" when it resolves.\[1\]
- Phone and laptop speakers reproduce little below about 300 Hz.\[16\]
- Mastercard spent months on a melody that "resonates globally while maintaining local relevance"; amp notes some sonic logos fail to gain traction in other cultures.\[36\]\[37\]

## Audio logos

- **Netflix.** Lon Bender's wedding ring knocking on a bedroom cabinet, sweetened with a slowed anvil and muted hits;\[2\]\[3\] the "blossom" is a reversed, processed electric-guitar phrase by Charlie Campagna.\[3\] It had to be short, "more cinematic than electronic," and create tension and resolve it "in like three seconds";\[3\]\[4\] a "call and response" that "builds up tension and then releases it, just like a story does."\[2\]\[8\] Bender avoided any instrument "played straight up" because it "was too specific."\[8\] Twenty Thousand Hertz reports "an entire year of countless versions, iterations, first impressions." Bender's team made 20 to 30 options in aesthetic groups (musical, effects-based, hybrid), and Netflix played one candidate to each person in a blind survey of thousands and asked what words came to mind: it rejected a sound whose top word was "confused", and the winner's was "movie."\[8\] Hans Zimmer's longer theatrical version still ends in the unchanged ta-dum.\[8\] Todd Yellin: visual idents "go out of style a lot more often than sounds."\[8\]
- **Intel.** Five notes (D♭ D♭ G♭ D♭ A♭), a blend of synths, mostly xylophone and marimba.\[5\] Werzowa's brief was "reliability, innovation and trust."\[25\] In a 60-person focus group, 80% recognised the melody on a violin and 100% with the proper sound, even with a wrong note added.\[6\]
- **Google's Super G**: two piano notes an octave apart in G, "flammed" for playfulness, under 1.5 s.\[1\]
- **Mastercard** (newsroom, 2024): a 30-second melody, "a three-second version that punctuates its advertising and a 1.6-second burst that plays when shoppers pay", at "more than 500 million points of interaction", with hundreds of stylistic variations so the logo can "feel at home" at "a football match or…an opera."\[36\]\[26\]\[27\]\[63\]
- **Length:** melodic audio logos typically run 2 to 3 s on 3 to 5 notes; textural ones 1 to 2 s, at the risk of being heard as a sound effect.\[22\] Mastercard's in-ad signature is about 3 s.\[23\]
- **Memorability:** a 2026 study of 90 unreleased commercial sonic logos (Silas, Müllensiefen et al., n = 1,860) found simpler melodies easier to sing back (recall) and more complex ones easier to recognise. Tonality predicted neither.\[7\]
- **amp's** 2026 Best Audio Brands report warns that many brands are "starting to sound the same."\[64\]\[65\]

## ElevenLabs sound effects

- Text to sound effect, with an optional duration from 0.1 to 30 s (a set duration costs 40 credits per second), looping for ambiences, and a prompt influence (high is literal, low varies more).\[38\]\[39\]\[40\] Output is MP3, and WAV at 48 kHz for non-looping effects.\[38\] SFX v2 (September 2025) raised the length from 22 to 30 s and the sample rate from 44.1 to 48 kHz, and added looping.\[41\]\[42\]
- The docs list terms the model understands (impact, whoosh, ambience, one-shot, loop, stem, braam, glitch, drone) and accept musical specs like "90 BPM" or "Vintage brass stabs in F minor."\[38\]
- Each generation returns several variants, which can differ a lot.\[43\]
- ElevenLabs' help centre: complex prompts with several events work, "but for the best results… generate individual sound effects and then combine them in an audio editor." It suggests phrases like "high-quality, professionally recorded… sound effects foley."\[44\]\[45\]
- Limits: no precise pitch or tempo, results vary between runs, occasional artifacts and extra events, and weak results on very specific real sounds (Tom's Guide: it failed at a 1990s modem, adding too much musical tone).\[43\]
- Google's sound team, with its own generative models, found generated audio most valuable "as raw material to be manipulated or as a mood board."\[1\]

### Licence

- Free plan: non-commercial only, with attribution.\[46\]\[47\] Paid plans: content generated during a paid subscription can be used commercially indefinitely, subject to each service's terms;\[48\] beta services are excluded.\[49\]
- The Prohibited Use Policy bans "selling… distributing… or commercially using or exploiting any Output… generated using our Sound Effects product on a standalone basis," including "sound libraries."\[50\]
- The Sound Effects Terms (12 February 2026) allow ElevenLabs to sublicense outputs, "including making SFX Outputs available to other ElevenLabs users," unless the "Disable" setting is on; the opt-out is not retroactive.\[51\]

## Loudness and delivery

| Destination | Target | Status |
|---|---|---|
| YouTube, web, most social | about −14 LUFS integrated, at most −1 dBTP | YouTube publishes no number; Ian Shepherd's analysis of "Stats for nerds" shows it turns loud uploads down but "doesn't turn up quieter videos", and "it's not about aiming for -14 LUFS!"\[52\] Spotify officially normalises to −14 LUFS, "below −1dB TP."\[9\]\[53\]\[54\] |
| Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn | −14 LUFS as a default | No official specs; louder recommendations from blogs are unverified.\[53\]\[55\]\[56\] |
| Broadcast (EU) | −23 LUFS, −1 dBTP; adverts at most −18 LUFS short-term | EBU R128 and R128 s1\[10\]\[57\] |
| Broadcast (US) | −24 LKFS, −2 dBTP | ATSC A/85:2026-07\[11\]\[58\]\[59\] |
| Podcast | about −16 LUFS, at most −1 dBTP | Apple Podcasts\[60\] |

- With normalisation on, a more dynamic mix sounds bigger at the same playback level; over-limiting to hit a loud target flattens the impacts.\[61\]\[62\]
- Chrome: "Muted autoplay is always allowed"; sound needs prior interaction or engagement.\[12\]

## Caveats

- Some figures conflict or are secondhand. Mastercard's acceptance sound is 1.3 s in most interviews and 1.6 s in the 2024 newsroom piece,\[23\]\[36\] and its variation count 200+ or about 300 depending on the year.\[26\]\[69\] The Intel focus-group figures come from a secondary write-up, not an Intel publication.\[6\]
- Social platforms publish no loudness targets. The −14 LUFS figure is an industry measurement; claims that TikTok or Instagram prefer −10 to −12 LUFS are unverified.\[53\]\[56\]\[70\]
- ElevenLabs' terms and features change often; the terms quoted are dated February 2026. This is not legal advice, in particular on registering an AI-assisted sound as a trademark.
- Several numbers are practitioner rules of thumb, not standards: pre-delay 20 to 60 ms, UI sounds 50 to 200 ms, 3 to 6 hero hits in a launch video, the 300 Hz phone-speaker floor.
- The memorability study used piano transcriptions of unreleased logos, so recognition in the wild (with timbre, repetition and a picture) will differ.\[7\]
